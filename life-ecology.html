Squirrels & Pine Martens — Ecology on a Torus

A spatial contact process on a torus: two rival prey species compete for empty ground. Red squirrels (native) and grey squirrels (invasive) each colonise an adjacent empty cell at their own per-species birth rate β; an occupied cell survives each tick with probability σ.

Why grey tends to win

Grey's default β is set higher than red's — the invasive-species advantage. With no predation yet, a higher β simply out-colonises empty ground faster, so grey expands and red's share shrinks over time. Watch the two lines in the population chart cross.

The pine marten

A conserved predator (brown, drawn dim when hungry and bright when fed). Each tick it loses δ energy to metabolism and gains g by eating one adjacent prey. Energy caps at E_cap (15); a marten with energy ≤0 starves and dies. A well-fed marten (energy ≥ E_breed) can spill a new marten into an empty neighbour cell at rate μ, paying a breeding cost. Use Reintroduce martens to drop a sparse scatter onto the current grid, or pick Marten in Pen species to place them by hand.

Squirrelpox — the race the marten loses

Greys carry squirrelpox and are barely troubled by it; in reds it is almost always fatal. That is a second asymmetry, and it runs opposite to evasion: evasion favours the reds, the pox favours the greys. Which one wins is the whole question. Carriers are drawn with a sickly yellow-green tint — light on greys, who merely carry it, heavy on reds, who are dying.

The sim starts pox-free, which is Ireland — Irish greys largely do not carry it, and Ireland is where the marten recovery is cleanest. Press Introduce squirrelpox to get Britain. Everything you have watched so far was the easy version.

Now the result. Release martens into a pox-free landscape and the reds come back (~5800 on a 120×60). Introduce the pox first and the reds hit zero every time — at every release date tried, even releasing while 271 reds were still alive. And the martens are not failing: they still crash the greys to zero, and by destroying the reservoir they wipe out the pox itself (watch the carrier count fall to 0). They simply arrive too late. The epidemic sweeps in tens of ticks; martens need hundreds to spread. The rescue liberates an empty landscape.

Drop Transmission to 0.10 and the martens win again — the cliff is between 0.10 and 0.15 and is nearly vertical. Odder still: at 0.10, raising Lethal to reds saves more reds. A pox that kills in two ticks has two ticks to spread; one that lets its host linger has twenty. The deadlier strain burns itself out.

That bleak result assumes nowhere to hide. Turn the woodland on and it changes completely — see below.

The woodland, and the way out

The greys' advantage is an acorn-and-hazel advantage. It belongs to the broadleaf wood, not to the animal. So Terrain strength scales the grey's β by the ground it stands on: in broadleaf (brown) it climbs, in conifer (dark green) it collapses. Red's β never changes. Reds held the whole country before the greys came — they are outcompeted in broadleaf, not excluded from it. That matters enormously: it means ground the marten clears is ground the reds can walk straight back into. The marten, being a generalist, ignores terrain entirely.

So β_grey > β_red is not a fact about greys. It is a fact about broadleaf. Set the terrain to a conifer basin and the invasive advantage simply evaporates.

Now the three-act story. Pox, no refuge: the reds die everywhere (turn Terrain to 0 and watch). Pox plus a conifer refuge: the reds survive — but only inside it, penned, while the greys hold everything else. That is Britain today, and it is a stable stalemate: 1858 reds in the stronghold, zero outside, for as long as you care to run it. Pox, refuge, and martens: the greys collapse and the reds pour out — 3798 of them onto ground they had not touched in centuries.

The refuge does not even need to be big. A stronghold covering 5% of the map is enough to reseed the entire landscape once the martens do their work. It only has to exist. Protect the strongholds, restore the predator, and the rest follows — which is, more or less, the actual conservation strategy.

Founding a population

Martens breed only if another marten is adjacent (Breeding needs a mate), and they die at a small Background mortality whatever their energy — age, accidents, disease, the deaths an energy budget cannot express. Together these mean a single marten founds nothing: it lives out its life, eats a few neighbours, and dies. Drop Release size to 1 and watch it fail; the greys win regardless. That is why the real programme moved dozens of animals rather than one, and why the original goal was a viable population, not a lone predator.

Two honest caveats. The threshold here is a hard step — 1 fails, 2 nearly always succeeds — whereas real founder groups of two usually fail for reasons this model has no notion of: finding a mate across a whole valley, dispersal, roads, inbreeding. And because these martens cannot move, two released far apart can never pair up at all, so where you release them does work that roaming does in reality.

You can translocate the reds too — Reintroduce reds drops a healthy founder patch, the same way real programmes do. It matters enormously where it lands. Release reds into grey-held broadleaf and they are mopped up in a few dozen ticks; release them into a conifer refuge, or into ground the martens have just cleared, and they take and spread. So an extinction is not always final here — but only if you fix the habitat or the predator first. Reintroducing reds into the same broadleaf that killed them just feeds them to the greys again, which is exactly why real reinforcement is paired with grey control, not done alone.

Evasion — why any of this works

Reds co-evolved with the marten and evade it; greys are North American, ground-naive, and mostly do not. That single asymmetry is the whole mechanism. Let the greys take over, then hit Reintroduce martens: greys crash, reds recover.

The control case is one click. Make evasion symmetric sets red's evasion equal to grey's — same predator, same predation pressure, only the asymmetry removed. The reds die anyway. A predator that eats both species does not save the native one; it just harvests both. Predation alone is not the cause — the asymmetry is.

Watch the marten line too. Nothing couples it to the greys explicitly, but because hunting an evading red usually wastes the turn, martens can only live off greys — so their numbers track grey numbers, greys rebound as martens fade, and the pair settles into a cycle. That is emergent, not coded.

Other food — and why it changes everything

Real martens are generalists: voles, birds, eggs, berries, carrion. Their numbers do not depend on squirrels. Other food adds that. It has to be a shared resource — a flat food bonus would just be a rename of a lower δ, since e−δ+food is only e−(δ−food). So each marten's share is divided by how many martens crowd around it, which is what gives martens a baseline density set by the countryside rather than by squirrels. Set it to 0 to switch the subsidy off.

Turning it on stops the boom-bust cycle: a subsidised marten does not starve when the greys thin out, so the greys never get their rebound and are wiped out completely rather than cycling. That matches Ireland, where greys vanished from marten country. But push Other food past ~2.5 and the martens take the reds down too — a predator propped up by other food has no reason to stop. Ecologists call this hyperpredation; it is how cats fed by introduced rabbits have exterminated island birds.

The default of 2.0 is exactly twice δ, and that is deliberate. A marten shares its other food with its neighbours, so a pair gets 2.0/2 = δ and breaks even, while a trio gets 2.0/3 and starves. The pair is the stable unit; any bigger group has to be paid for in squirrels.

Now the strange one. Set Other food to 0 and raise Red evades to 0.95: the reds do worse, and the greys take over. With no other food, the marten lives only on squirrels — so reds that evade too well starve the very predator that is keeping the greys off them. Unsubsidised, the reds have to feed their own bodyguard. Only with both the other food and good evasion (the defaults) do you get the real outcome: greys gone, reds abundant, martens steady.

Sources & credits

This simulation is a teaching toy, not a research model. Every number in it — birth rates, evasion odds, transmission, forage — was tuned by hand until the behaviour matched the story the real studies tell. The numbers are illustrative; the mechanisms are drawn from the peer-reviewed work below, which is where the actual evidence lives. If any of this surprised you, read the real thing.

Ireland — where it was first seen

In the Irish midlands the pine marten recovered on its own, and the grey squirrel population collapsed across roughly 9,000 km² of its former range, letting red squirrels recolonise woods greys had held for thirty years. This is the clean case the sim reproduces when squirrelpox is switched off — because Irish greys are largely pox-free. Sheehy & Lawton (2014) is the founding paper.

Britain — the naïve grey

In Scotland and northern England, Sheehy, Sutherland, O'Reilly & Lambin (2018) — memorably titled “The enemy of my enemy is my friend” — found the mechanism the sim is built around. Where martens were well established, grey squirrels avoided feeders and declined, while reds did better. Greys are North American and have never co-evolved with the marten, so they do not recognise it as a threat; reds do. That single behavioural asymmetry — not the predator's appetite — is what turns the marten into an ally of the native. The predation dynamics themselves are detailed in Twining et al. (2020).

Squirrelpox — the reason Britain is harder than Ireland

Greys carry squirrelpox almost harmlessly; in reds it is nearly always fatal. Tompkins et al. (2003) showed that competition alone cannot explain how fast reds vanished — with the virus present, replacement runs up to about 25 times faster. That is the “race the marten loses” in the sim: introduce the pox before the martens arrive and the reds are gone before help exists. The virus itself was described by Sainsbury et al. (2000).

Refuges, and the marten that clears the disease too

Two findings the sim stumbled into turn out to be real. Twining et al. (2021) found declining greys persist in refugia — exactly the conifer-stronghold effect in the “Woodland” panel, where a small refuge reseeds the landscape once the greys are cleared. And Slade et al. (2022) modelled the marten suppressing both the grey and the shared virus — the same double effect you see when the carrier count falls to zero after a marten release.

Reintroductions in Wales & England

Unlike Ireland, martens in much of Britain had to be actively brought back. The Vincent Wildlife Trust ran the translocations into mid-Wales and the Forest of Dean — the real-world version of the “Reintroduce martens” button, and the reason it releases a group, not one animal. The BBC report is a plain-language way in.
