Conveyor Assembly

Belts carry parts past workers standing on either side. A worker grabs the parts it needs, assembles them over several ticks, then waits for an empty cell to put the finished product down. Every tick each belt shifts one cell right; whatever falls off the end is counted as waste, and a fresh cell arrives on the left.

The contested resource

Only one worker may touch a cell per tick. In the original assignment the north worker always wins — south only gets the cell if north did nothing with it. A worker that is mid-assembly does not need the cell, so it never blocks; and a blocked worker can still keep assembling.

Switch Cell priority to Alternate and watch the north/south split. North-first costs south about a fifth of its output and buys no extra throughput at all — the rule decides who does the work, not how much gets done.

Several belts

Up to ten belts run in parallel from one seeded stream. They do not interact, so output scales with belt count and efficiency — products per belt-movement — stays flat. That is the point of the number: it stays comparable when you add capacity.

Several products

Product mix swaps in a different set of parts and recipes. Products may use entirely separate parts, or compete for a shared one, or differ in how many parts they take. A product is itself just a part, so a recipe can consume another recipe's output.

Worker specialisation

Assign each worker one product and it will only pick up that product's parts — it lets everything else ride past. Two results worth finding:

  • Dedicated lines cost about half your throughput — unless you dedicate the supply too. The feed is undifferentiated, so half of what arrives on a dedicated belt is parts its workers refuse to touch, and it rides straight off the end.
  • What specialisation buys is control of the mix. With two products competing for a shared part, generalists crowd one out (roughly 2:1 on the shared preset). Specialists split it evenly, for a few percent of throughput.

By belt with fewer belts than products leaves the later products with nobody assigned, so they are never built at all. The panel warns when that happens.

Try the just-in-time supply

The Just-in-time option replaces the random feed with a perfect delivery — exactly the parts needed, in order, no gaps ever. Throughput collapses to almost nothing.

That is not a fault. A finished product needs an empty cell to leave on, and a supply that never sends a gap never provides one, so every worker ends up permanently holding a product it cannot put down. The idle cells in the random feed are not waste — they are the output channel. A real line needs outbound capacity as much as it needs parts, and any delivery schedule worth the name has to reserve some.

Reproducibility and sweeps

One seeded generator drives everything, so the same seed and settings replay the same run exactly — in the browser and under node --test. Copy setup puts the configuration on the clipboard; Load setup takes it back. Sweep runs the model headlessly across belt lengths and assembly times and prints the efficiency surface to the console — a surface, not a picture, is what the model is actually for.

How it is built

The rules live in life-conveyor-core.js, a pure module with no DOM, shared with the Node tests in life-conveyor-core.test.js. Parts are integers and recipes are data — {out, inputs, time} — so more subparts, more products, multi-stage assembly and alternative delivery policies are configuration, not new code.

Ported from a 2018 conveyor assignment, originally Python (simulate_conveyor.py), whose three correctness invariants — component conservation, worker-state validity and cell mutual exclusion — are checked here on every boot and pinned by the test suite.
